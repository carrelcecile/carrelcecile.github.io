ASEAN

Tensions between Thailand and Cambodia

Published 2025 · International Crisis Group

Tensions between Thailand and Cambodia have soared since a deadly border clash. Both sides have deployed more troops and exchanged bellicose rhetoric. Thailand’s Constitutional Court suspended Prime Minister Paetongtarn Shinawatra from office on 1 July, as the political fallout from a rancorous border dispute between Thailand and Cambodia spreads. The court suspended Paetongtarn after taking up a petition submitted by 36 senators, who argued that she had committed ethical violations breaching the country’s constitution in a 15 June phone call with former Cambodian prime minister Hun Sen, which was leaked three days later.

Read more

The timing and nature of the call were charged with political significance, coming amid a precipitous downturn in bilateral relations sparked by a 28 May clash between Cambodian and Thai troops along an undemarcated stretch of the border.

Cambodia has taken the matter to the ICJ, seeking a ruling on disputed territory. Thailand rejects the court’s jurisdiction, insisting disputes be resolved bilaterally.

Miscalculation or provocation could easily deepen hardship and lead to further loss of life. With border closures and rising political pressure, local communities and cross-border livelihoods are increasingly at risk.

Myanmar’s exiled journalists

Published May 2025 · International Crisis Group

More than 100 journalists were detained within the first three months of Myanmar’s 2021 coup, as the military cracked down on the country’s independent media.

Read more

Since the takeover, northern Thailand has become an uneasy base for journalists seeking to operate beyond the junta’s reach. Independent media outlets exiled from Myanmar play a vital role in reporting on the civil war and the destruction caused by natural disasters—while shaping the political culture of the resistance.

They face considerable pressure, as any perceived criticism of anti-regime forces is seen as undermining the revolutionary cause. Though underground reporters remain inside Myanmar, few experienced journalists can risk returning due to arrest and detention by the military. The biggest challenge is a mounting financial crunch. With the traditional funding model gone, outlets now rely almost entirely on international donors.

The Philippines’ military modernisation

Published August 2025 · International Crisis Group · Read the report

The Philippines is at a strategic inflection point. Sitting at the fulcrum of Asian geopolitics, its military is shifting from domestic-focused security challenges to growing external conflict risks.

Read more

China is becoming more assertive in the South China Sea. It is expanding its territorial claims, inhibiting access to its own Exclusive Economic Zone, and creating friction with the U.S.

Located between the South China Sea and the Pacific Ocean, the Philippines’ threat perceptions have spiked. In response, it is accelerating efforts to modernise its military and cultivate its security partnerships with Washington and others. It is also expanding ties with other countries to draw even with more militarily advanced neighbours and hedge against scenarios in which the White House downgrades its commitment to its Asian allies.

Come what may, Manila wants greater deterrent and defensive capabilities to help discourage Chinese expansionism in the South China Sea. But balancing deterrence with diplomacy is key. Increasingly dangerous confrontations at sea risk more strategic distrust and even armed conflict.

Ensuring good communications with Beijing is vital, including by strengthening crisis channels to help manage escalatory risks when incidents occur.

China and the Indo-Pacific

The US and China are reshaping the Indo-Pacific security architecture

Published May 2025 · International Crisis Group · Read the report

The U.S. and China are reshaping the Indo-Pacific security architecture. As China rises, and the U.S. sends mixed messages about its intentions, Beijing and Washington should resume strategic talks to manage the risks of unintended escalation.

Read more

Asia has experienced its largest-ever military spending increase in the last decade, fuelling regional “militarisation”. The intensifying U.S.-China military competition is shaping threat perceptions in Asian capitals, leading many countries to revisit their defence postures and doctrines. The U.S. trade war is also impeding diplomacy that might manage these dynamics.

Continued militarisation raises the probability of unintended collisions and accidents that involve either the U.S. or one of its allies and partners, on one side, and China on the other.

Europe

European security at the latest NATO summit

Published June 2025 · International Crisis Group

European security has not looked so fraught in decades. As NATO meets, European governments are working out how to maintain support for Ukraine and ensure their own security in the long term in ways that do not require the United States as a bulwark.

Read more

NATO and the EU remain central to their responses, but they have also been slowed by consensus decision-making, bureaucracy and, in NATO’s case, the integral role played by the United States. European countries are seeking new ways to make cooperation among themselves, and with others, faster, easier, more credible and more effective.

The result has been a patchwork of initiatives. Some are tied to NATO and the EU, while others are not. Sub-regional defence groupings, capability coalitions, and the idea of a reassurance force all reflect an evolving strategy. However, ad hoc formats caused confusion: participation varies, and disagreement persists. Such coalitions signal their commitment, ostensibly even without U.S. backing in some cases.

Europe and the US at the UN

Published 2025 · International Crisis Group

European policymakers now need to navigate a UN system in which the U.S. is a potential adversary.

Read more

On the war’s third anniversary, the U.S. abandoned its coordination with Ukraine and the EU, blocking Kyiv’s resolution and putting forward its own text. Unlike past resolutions, Washington’s proposal omitted any mention of Russian aggression or Ukrainian sovereignty, instead calling simply for rapid progress toward peace.

Many non-Western states that previously backed Kyiv stayed neutral, suggesting European countries will find it harder to sustain diplomatic support as divisions at the UN deepen. As the war continues, this shift could reshape future negotiations and international responses.

International conferences

Libya sanctions at the UN

International Crisis Group

The UN should not miss the chance to make further reforms to the Libya sanctions regime.

Read more

In 2011 during Libya’s civil war, the UN Security Council imposed an asset freeze on Libya’s sovereign wealth fund with the aim of preventing the Qadhafi regime from plundering the fund, estimated then at over $60 billion. Since then, the Council has long been reluctant to relax its sanctions, given Libya’s tumult and its lack of confidence in the Libyan Investment Authority.

However, in early 2025, it finally acceded to some requests by Libyan authorities.

These reforms are a good start, but the Council should not wait for resolution of Libya’s political crisis to make additional changes to its sanctions regime. Even if there is no immediate resolution of the country’s political divisions, there are ways to ensure that increasing the fund’s size and protecting it from misuse. The Libyan Investment Authority should also take steps to bolster its credibility.

Washington’s threats to decertify Colombia

Published June 2025 · International Crisis Group

Expectations are running high in both Bogotá and Washington that the country could be decertified in 2025 for its supposed failure to meet Washington’s drug policy goals, ending a three-decade run of compliance. A large part of U.S. aid to Colombia depends on the annual certification process, which determines whether the country is meeting Washington’s criteria for satisfactory cooperation in fighting illicit drugs.

Read more

If decertified, Colombia stands to lose hundreds of millions of dollars in military and police aid, face punishing economic sanctions and visa restrictions, and see a freeze in longstanding political ties. The U.S. would likely feel repercussions as well, since Colombia provides Washington with crucial intelligence as well as forces primed to carry out joint operations.

Trump’s threat to impose crippling tariffs on Colombia in January over migration flights sent a message to other regional leaders about the high cost of non-compliance with his deportation demands. Extradition requests could also disrupt relations.

Decertification would open the door to other geopolitical influences in Colombia, to the detriment of U.S. influence. Withdrawing U.S. funds would mean the security forces could lose what power they still possess to restrain Colombia’s surging violence. “The main beneficiaries of decertification would be criminal groups,” one defence official argued.

The Central African Republic’s transhumance-related violence

Published May 2025 · International Crisis Group

Ten years of crisis have profoundly disrupted the livestock sector and exacerbated conflicts between nomadic herders and sedentary farmers, accentuated since the 2013 coup and the formation of armed groups.

Read more

The government, which has treated transhumance primarily as a security problem, has been unable to curb the violence. International actors have sought to address it, yet responses remain fragmented and poorly coordinated.

The livestock sector is a pillar of the Central African economy. It remains underexploited due to the violent climate and lack of pastoral infrastructure. Unlocking its potential would allow the state to generate tax revenue, essential for long-term development.

The country’s improving security situation, coupled with easing tensions with Chad, presents an opportunity to make rural stability a political priority ahead of the 2025 elections.

The European Union (EU) Watchlist entries identify ten countries facing deadly conflict, humanitarian emergency, or other crises. In these places, early action, driven or supported by the EU and its member states, could save lives and enhance prospects for stability. Each year, Crisis Group publishes two updates to the EU Watch List identifying where the EU and its member states can enhance prospects for peace.

Bangladesh. With elections approaching, Bangladesh’s interim government is under pressure to deliver on its promised reforms. In this excerpt from the Watch List 2025, Crisis Group highlights actions the EU and its member states can take to support the country’s democratic transition.

Ukraine. With Russia’s all-out invasion entering its fourth year, Ukraine continues to face battlefield challenges while prospects of a peace deal remain uncertain. In this excerpt from the Watch List 2025, Crisis Group explores how the EU can support Kyiv and maintain European security.

Moldova. A severe energy crisis is putting increasing strain on Moldova, particularly its breakaway region Transnistria. In this excerpt from the Watch List 2025, Crisis Group lays out ways the EU can help the country get through the winter and prepare for autumn elections.

Syria. With the Assad regime gone, Syria is beginning its recovery from years of civil war and autocratic rule. In this excerpt from the Watch List 2025, Crisis Group explains what the EU can do to keep the country moving in the right direction.
